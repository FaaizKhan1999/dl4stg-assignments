\section*{Task 1 --- Forecasting Across Heterogeneous Sensors}

\subsection*{0. The World and Forecasting Setup}

I will use this section specifically to outline about what I interpret as the setup of the problem in this task, for my own understanding.

There is a production line with 4 rotating production stations connected in series, labelled 1--4. Each station has one vibration accelerometer --- a physical sensor attached to the station to measure its physical movement in metres per second squared ($m/s^2$) --- sampled at 20 Hz, equivalent to 0.05 seconds per sample.

While the samples are taken at the exact time intervals regularly, the readings itself are not exactly accurate and are the sum of three components:

$$y_i(t) = b_i(t) + s_i(t) + \epsilon_i(t)$$

The above equation is station $i$'s accelerometer reading at sample $t$. The components are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $b_i(t)$: The slowly varying baseline caused by load/control conditions and sensor calibration drift. These vary on a 12-second time-scale.
  \item $s_i(t)$: The dominating, repeating physical vibration of the rotating station.
  \item $\epsilon_i(t)$: The small, independent observation noise.
\end{itemize}

The time is divided into operating runs: 192 samples per run. The first run produces product A, the second produces product B, the third produce product C, and then the process repeats. Each product has a characteristic operating-speed range, or the period of its vibration: 14--18, 21--27, and 28--36 samples respectively. This means that the cycle durations are between 0.70--1.80 seconds. At the start of each run, some factors determine one actual period within the corresponding product's range, which is shared by all 4 stations for the duration of the run.

For this task, the context length $L = 96$ and the forecast horizon $H = 48$. At forecast origin $t$, a model observes the previous $L$ readings and predicts the next $H$ readings.

$$X_i = \{y_i(t - L), \hdots, y_i(t - 1)\} \in \mathbb{R}^L, \hat{Y}_i = \{\hat{y}_i(t), \hdots, \hat{y}_i(t + H - 1)\} \in \mathbb{R}^H$$

The above would be the input and output sequences for station $i$. A forecast origin is only eligible if the entire context and target lie within an operating run: valid forecast origins for each run are from sample 96 to sample 144 (assuming zero-indexing).

The data is split into a train-validation-test split out of a total of 15360 samples. Stations 1--3 are established stations whereas station 4 is held out and serves as the unseen-station test case.

The last observed value in each context is used as the anchor which is subtracted from every single sample within that window. This means that the last sample will be 0 and forces every window to end at 0, effectively deleting any long term calibration offsets. The anchored values are scaled by the reciprocal of a single, constant scale value which is determined at the start using training data from stations 1--3 (station 4 and the validation/test regions do not contribute to this scale). This normalizes the data and compresses the amplitude so the neural network receives standardized values. The predictions are then transformed back to physical units before evaluation.

\textbf{The Inductive Bias of Moving Averages} \\
A moving average is a highly sensible inductive bias for this specific problem because it directly mirrors the physical reality of the production line. We know the signal consists of a slow 12-second drift (240 samples) overlaid with a rapid 0.7-to-1.8-second vibration (14--36 samples). The moving average acts as a low-pass filter, explicitly forcing the neural network to view the slow baseline and the fast recurring cycles separately rather than demanding it untangle a single, messy composite signal. 

The training-only oracle diagnostic is crucial here because the model is only ever fed the combined, noisy signal $y_i(t)$. Without the oracle, we would simply be guessing the width $m$ based on downstream loss. By using the known synthetic period during training, the oracle directly measures how well our chosen width isolates the true underlying frequency, allowing for a targeted hyperparameter choice.

\textbf{Limitations of this Bias} \\
This specific inductive bias would fail if the sensor experienced an abrupt, instantaneous level shift---such as a sudden physical shock to the machine or a hard reset of the sensor calibration. A centered moving average assumes that the baseline changes smoothly over time. If it encounters a sudden step-change, the fixed-width averaging window will smear that sharp jump into a gradual slope, corrupting both the extracted trend and the residual vibration signal.

\textbf{The Benefit of Decomposition} \\
Decomposing the context first makes the delay evidence significantly cleaner. The raw signal is heavily dominated by the slow baseline drift; because a slow ramp remains numerically similar to itself across many shifts, the raw signal correlation forms a wide, uninformative hump. This strongly supports the delay mixer's inductive bias, as it allows the model to easily identify and aggregate information from these distinct, highly-correlated shifted periods without being confused by the underlying drift.

\textbf{Limitations of Delay Mixing} \\
The assumption that useful temporal relationships are organized by recurring displacements fails when dealing with an isolated transient event, such as a sudden, one-off mechanical shock. Because a transient event happens only once, it has no cyclical spacing and does not repeat. A delay mixer, which constructs forecasts by looking back at fixed lag intervals and adding shifted copies of the sequence, would either entirely miss the isolated event or erroneously duplicate the random shock across the forecast horizon at repeating intervals.

\section*{Task 2 --- Leaderboard Challenge}

\subsection*{Methodology and Autoformer Architecture}

The core forecasting engine utilizes the Autoformer architecture, originally proposed by Wu et al. (2021) in \textit{Autoformer: Decomposition Transformers with Auto-Correlation for Long-Term Series Forecasting}. Unlike standard Transformers which rely on self-attention to calculate pointwise dependencies, the Autoformer is specifically designed with an inductive bias for time-series forecasting. It abandons vanilla self-attention in favor of an Auto-Correlation mechanism, which discovers sub-series level connections by measuring temporal delays and aggregating similar historical segments. 

To address the high volatility and specific temporal dynamics of the dataset, several critical structural modifications were implemented on top of the base architecture:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Series Decomposition:} Following the original paper, the architecture employs a SeriesDecomposition block utilizing a centered moving-average filter to split the input sequence into a slow-moving trend and a cyclical remainder (seasonal) stream. This decomposition is interleaved throughout both the encoder and decoder layers, allowing the network to isolate and progressively accumulate the trend while utilizing the Auto-Correlation mixer exclusively on the repeating seasonal patterns.
    
    \item \textbf{Reversible Instance Normalization (RevIN):} To handle the massive baseline shifts and heavy-tailed nature of the data, the model incorporates \texttt{RevIN} (Kim et al., 2022). This mechanism normalizes the target channel of each input window independently to a zero-mean, unit-variance distribution before processing. After the decoder predicts the forecast horizon, the exact inverse transformation is applied to restore the original physical scale. To ensure numerical stability on completely flat, quiet context windows, a variance epsilon factor ($\epsilon=1e-5$) was enforced strictly inside the standard deviation calculation.
    
    \item \textbf{Continuous Cyclic Auto-Correlation:} The AutoCorrelationMixer replaces standard multi-head attention. It computes temporal delays via Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) in $O(L \log L)$ time. A critical architectural fix was required in the cross-attention block: rather than padding the encoder's shorter output sequence with zeros, the aggregate\_delays function applies a modulo operator over the unpadded encoder length. This enforces a continuous, repeating cyclic sample for the future horizon, preventing the decoder from gathering empty zero-tensors that previously caused the model to collapse to the context mean.
    
    \item \textbf{Progressive Trend Accumulation \& Covariate Injection:} The decoder utilizes progressive trend accumulation across its sub-layers. It takes the last observed trend value from the context window as its initialization anchor, avoiding artificial baseline boundary drops. Furthermore, known future covariates (the binary regime indicators and \texttt{feature\_D}) are linearly projected and injected directly into the decoder's embedding space at the corresponding horizon positions, providing explicit temporal conditioning.
\end{itemize}

The final deployed model utilized a context length of 336, a model dimension of 32 with 4 attention heads, 2 encoder layers, and 1 decoder layer, yielding a total trainable parameter count ($P$) of 69,383. This specific configuration was trained for 15 epochs using MSE loss to maximize stability against the heavy-tailed variance before early stopping triggered.

\subsection*{Score Optimization and Leaderboard Submissions}

The competitive leaderboard penalizes parameter count ($P$), but the scoring formula heavily prioritizes raw RMSE. Therefore, the optimization strategy focused primarily on structural architectural improvements rather than arbitrarily limiting model capacity. This progression is reflected in the three main submissions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attempt 1 (RMSE: 116.43, $P$: 7,938, $E$: 4):} The initial Autoformer setup collapsed to predicting a nearly flat line near the median. This was driven by a zero-padding bug in the cross-attention mechanism, where the decoder gathered empty zero-tensors for the future horizon, and the $L_1$ loss mathematically forced the model to seek the median of the heavy-tailed distribution rather than tracking the high-variance spikes.
    \item \textbf{Attempt 2 (RMSE: 94.83, $P$: 57,863, $E$: 17):} To address the collapse, the cross-attention \texttt{aggregate\_delays} function was corrected to apply a modulo operator over the unpadded encoder length, ensuring a continuous, repeating cyclic sample. Additionally, numerical stability was introduced to the RevIN module by placing the epsilon term strictly inside the variance calculation, preventing floating-point noise explosions on completely flat context windows. These mathematical fixes, alongside an increase in model dimension ($d_{model}=32$) as well as switching the loss to MSE, allowed the model to actively track the spikes, dropping the RMSE by over 21 points.
    \item \textbf{Attempt 3 (RMSE: 78.06, $P$: 69,383, $E$: 15):} The final optimization targeted the temporal bottleneck. Time-series models utilizing Fast Fourier Transforms require sufficient historical lookback to resolve frequencies effectively. The context window was expanded from 96 to 336 steps (double the 168-step horizon), providing the Auto-Correlation mixer with two full operational cycles. Combined with a single decoder layer to prevent over-accumulation of the trend and a smaller batch size to smooth heavy-tailed gradient variance, this wide-context configuration yielded a massive improvement, dropping the final RMSE to 78.06 and the MAE to 50.92.
\end{itemize}
